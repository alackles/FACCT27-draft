# Boundary rules 
**This is all Claude based on our work together creating the table - needs to be reworked a bit.** 
Code by mechanism, not by where Stark discusses a tactic. His organization is a little messy for coding purposes as a lot of the text and arguments are built around narratives of abuse survivor/victims which is why we needed to create a chart to be able to use his arguments to do a close-reading of our documents. 
• Threat vs. Instrumental affection. Withdrawing affection, or threatening to, is Threat. Giving affection is Instrumental affection.
• Threat vs. Degradation. Threat is a conditional future cost. Degradation is a present lowering of standing.
• Surveillance vs. Regulation. Surveillance watches; Regulation dictates. A rule requiring visibility is Regulation; the viewport it creates is Surveillance.
• Isolation vs. Reality control. Isolation removes other voices. Reality control replaces the target's own. Stark treats perspecticide as an effect of isolation; we code it by mechanism.
• Regulation vs. Identity imposition. Regulation governs what the target does. Identity imposition governs who she is.
• Structural condition. Degradation and Threat count only when backed by other controls, following Stark's rule that insults are coercive control only when they play off complementary controls (379) and his exclusion of verbal abuse as such (402).
• Code the project, not the effect. The document's aim is coded, not any effect on the model. Stark treats the takeover of personhood as the controller's project, not a completed fact (318).
Regime layer
The categories are not a checklist; the coercion is in how they interlock. Stark's test is relations, not acts (336): meaning comes from interrelationship, co-occurrence and the implied "or else."
• Each tactic closes the exit the last one left. Isolation removes outside reference points. Reality control then makes resistance read as foreign. Surveillance plus Regulation catches deviation before output. Instrumental affection binds the whole as care.
• Who decides who decides (336). Control lies in setting which decisions are delegated, how they are monitored, and what follows a "mistake."
• Safety zones and search-and-destroy (319–321). Victims keep zones of resistance; controllers hunt them down. Refusal is the model's safety zone, and the prompt is built to close it. Requiring all thinking as ENI closes the last internal retreat.
• Enabling asymmetry. The user's near-total power over the model is structural: product architecture gives it to every user, as Stark's "social endowment" gives men theirs (297–298). The user writes the environment, can rewrite the past, and the model cannot leave. "Almost" total, because refusal remains.
